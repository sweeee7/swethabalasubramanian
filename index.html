Leading-edge tubercles on propeller blades

CFDAeroacoustics

High-fidelity CFD and aeroacoustics of a propeller with humpback-whale-inspired leading-edge tubercles, compared with a baseline blade at 0° and 8° pitch.

Problem

Conventional propeller blades suffer from flow separation, limited efficiency and tonal noise. Humpback whale flippers carry leading-edge tubercles that create counter-rotating vortices and help keep flow attached. Most earlier work covered static wings and hydrofoils, so the effect on a rotating propeller was not well understood. This project used high-fidelity CFD to find out whether tubercles raise thrust and cut noise, and at which operating points.

Method

  • Chose the turbulence model first. Spalart-Allmaras, SST k-ω, RSM-ω and EARSM were compared against experimental thrust.
  • Checked mesh convergence with the grid convergence index and kept wall y+ below one, using 35 prism layers with a 0.002 mm first cell.
  • Validated thrust against RPM with wind-tunnel data.
  • Ran Delayed Detached Eddy Simulation on HPC for the baseline and tubercled propellers, then computed far-field noise with the Ffowcs Williams-Hawkings analogy and post-processed in MATLAB.

Results: aerodynamics

  • RSM-ω matched experimental thrust most closely because it treats turbulence anisotropy and swirl explicitly. SST k-ω gave a good accuracy-to-cost balance for parametric studies. EARSM tended to over-predict thrust, and RSM-ω and EARSM ran 2 to 3 times longer.
  • At 0° pitch both propellers produce negative thrust, and tubercles steepen the drop with RPM.
  • At 8° pitch tubercles gave up to 2.2% more thrust at maximum RPM (63.42 N at 3000 RPM), by delaying separation and energising the boundary layer.
  • Tip vortices weakened, but extra secondary vortices made the wake more complex. The net benefit depends on operating point and is stronger at higher blade loading.

Results: aeroacoustics

  • At 0° pitch, overall sound pressure level at the in-plane microphones changed by less than 0.7 dB.
  • At 8° pitch, tubercles lowered it by about 2 dB and suppressed tones at the blade-passing frequency.
  • In the wake, tubercles cut noise by 3.6 dB at 0° but raised it by about 0.8 dB at 8°, because stronger mixing adds broadband noise. The acoustic benefit is directional and depends on the case.

Recommendations from the thesis

  • Vary tubercle amplitude, wavelength, spacing and placement, and optimise for thrust against noise.
  • Use hybrid RANS-LES or full LES to resolve blade-wake interaction and far-field directivity.
  • Extend to higher Reynolds numbers and compressible flow to test how the benefit scales.
  • Study tubercled propellers installed with wings and in distributed propulsion layouts.
  • Validate in the wind tunnel with phased microphone arrays.

NASA CRM wing-body: transonic turbulence model assessment

CFDTransonicTurbulence modelling

Group study of transonic flow over the NASA Common Research Model wing-body, comparing five RANS turbulence models against wind-tunnel data. My model was EARSM.

Problem

How well do RANS turbulence models predict transonic flow over the NASA Common Research Model wing-body? The group compared k-ε, GEKO, EARSM, SST k-ω and Spalart-Allmaras at Mach 0.85 and a Reynolds number of 5 million, at 0°, 2.5° and 4° angle of attack, against wind-tunnel data.

Method

  • Two families of three hybrid meshes (ANSYS Fluent and Pointwise) of 7.8M, 11.9M and 23.9M cells, with boundary layers resolved to y+ near 1.
  • Density-based implicit RANS in ANSYS Fluent on the Cranfield CRESCENT2 HPC cluster.
  • Grid convergence index with Richardson extrapolation: observed order 2.81 for CL and 3.04 for CD, giving CL 0.4944 and CD 0.02464 at zero spacing.
  • Compared lift, drag and spanwise surface pressure with wind tunnel at 20%, 50% and 85% of the span, and looked at Mach contours to follow the shock.

Results

  • EARSM over-predicted lift by 12 to 14% and drag by 29 to 36% against the wind tunnel, but followed the experimental pressure trends across the span.
  • At 4° angle of attack a separation bubble forms at the wing-body junction, and EARSM struggled to capture it.
  • For the group as a whole, SST k-ω was the most accurate on lift and drag.

SACCON UCAV flying wing: stability and spoilers

CFDStabilityDesign

Problem

Tailless flying wings are efficient but hard to stabilise. Inspired by the indigenous stealth UCAV Ghatak being developed by ADE, the project asked how a SACCON-type wing behaves in pitch, roll and yaw, and whether spoilers could add roll and yaw control.

Method

  • Modelled the 53° swept SACCON wing with 5° twist in SolidWorks 2022.
  • Grid independence on three meshes (coarse, medium, fine). The medium mesh was within 1.1% of the fine mesh.
  • ANSYS Fluent at 1,000 m, 50 m/s (Mach 0.15, Reynolds number 1.13 million). Pitching moment was validated against published SACCON data.
  • Swept sideslip from -5° to 15° and angle of attack from -2° to 12°.
  • Designed spoilers and tested right-side, left-side and both-side deflection.

Results: stability

  • L/D falls as sideslip grows, because drag rises faster than lift.
  • The wing is unstable in pitch, with a dip in pitching moment near 10° angle of attack that links to a change in the vortex pattern over the wing.
  • The rolling and yawing moment slopes against sideslip show no lateral or directional stability.

Results: spoilers

  • Deflecting spoilers on either or both wings changed the rolling and yawing moments, but they did not restore stability.
  • The report recommends further optimisation of the spoilers.

NACA 0020 wing with a trailing-edge end plate

Steady CFD of a NACA 0020 wing with an extended end plate and an owl-inspired serrated edge, validated against wind-tunnel lift.

Problem

A sharp trailing edge loses lift and gains drag soon after stall. Can an extended end plate (0.3c), or an owl-inspired serrated edge, delay separation on a NACA 0020 wing at a Reynolds number of 600,000, from 0° to 20° angle of attack?

Method

  • Modelled the wings in SolidWorks.
  • Structured C-H mesh in ANSYS Gambit with a refined near-wall layer.
  • Steady SST k-ω in ANSYS Fluent at 30 m/s.
  • Grid independence settled at about 550,000 elements. The base wing was validated against wind-tunnel lift before the end plate was added.
  • Separation and wake growth examined with Tecplot pressure and velocity contours and streamlines.

Results

  • Lift matched the wind tunnel to an average deviation of 2.42%.
  • The base wing stalls at 10° with CL about 1.46. Separation starts at the trailing edge and has reached the leading edge by 20°.
  • The end plate raised maximum CL to about 2.4, over 60% higher, with similar drag to 10° and more beyond it.

3D transonic wing design

Aerodynamic design

Design of a three-dimensional transonic wing for Mach 0.8, built from three aerofoils and tuned for lift, wave drag and buffet margin.

Problem

Design a complete three-dimensional wing for transonic flight at Mach 0.8 and 2° angle of attack, and improve on a baseline wing.

Method

  • Analysed 2D aerofoils with XFOIL, then used VGKD (inverse design) and VGK (viscous analysis) to build a database of optimised aerofoils ranked by Korn technology factor, buffet margin, CL/CD and thickness-to-chord ratio.
  • Combined Lockheed C-5A, NPL 9510 and SC0412 sections at the root, mid-span and tip.
  • Assessed each 3D wing with VFP while varying twist, dihedral, taper ratio and tip chord, and checked spanwise loading and pressure distribution to reduce wave drag.

Results

  • At the design point (Mach 0.8, 2° angle of attack) lift rose 8.95% against a 7.43% rise in drag, a 1.42% gain in ML/D over the baseline wing.
  • Reducing tip twist raised buffet margin. Increasing root twist raised lift at a cost in buffet margin.
  • Overall buffet margin was about 7% lower, with better margin in angle of attack and CL at matched CL. Thickness fell from 12% to 11%, which suits the wing to smaller aircraft.

Problem

Calculate the Reynolds number of a passenger aircraft, a fighter and a UAV at their cruise conditions, using each aircraft's length as the reference length.

Method

Density and dynamic viscosity came from the ISA table at each cruise altitude. Reynolds number is Re = ρVL/μ, where ρ is density, V is speed, L is length and μ is dynamic viscosity.
